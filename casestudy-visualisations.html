The brief

Most local plans are still PDFs.

England's local plans contain some of the most consequential data in the built environment, determining where homes, schools, and businesses are built. Yet they are almost universally presented as dense, inaccessible PDFs. The design challenge was not just technical: it was about creating a shared visual language for planning data that could work consistently across hundreds of different local authorities, each with different tools, capabilities, and teams. This discovery set out to understand what that standard needed to look like.

Three how might we questions
01
How might we move from PDFs to more visual, web-based local plans?
02
How might we meet the needs of all end users to navigate, engage with and interact with local plan content at every stage?

How can we improve the use of interactive policy maps to visualise local plan content?

Design journey

From whiteboard to live government service.

01
Ideation & co-design
Facilitated co-design sessions with local planning authorities, planning officers, and statutory consultees to explore how policy data is understood and accessed. Used sketches, paper prototypes, and workshop activities to surface user needs and navigation behaviours.
02
Colour systems & visual language
Translated research into a visual language for planning data: defining colour standards for policy themes, map layer hierarchy, and content structure. All WCAG-validated and designed to work consistently across hundreds of different local plans. These outputs became published national guidance in February 2025.
03
Prototyping & testing
Built and tested four prototypes spanning information architecture, interactive policy mapping, design standards, and data guidance. Conducted usability testing across three sessions with five local planning authorities of varying digital maturity.
04
Interactive map design
Designed the end-to-end interactive policy map, including Regulation 18, 19, and Adopted states, six policy themes, spatial constraints, search and filtering, polygon tools, and policy detail views.

Testing insights

Three things testing changed.

01
Some data belongs in a list, not on a map
A key design pivot: policies that apply to an entire area shouldn't be visualised as a map layer at all. They should appear as a list when a user searches a location. A small distinction that completely changed how the map interaction model worked.
02
A shared visual language was not just possible, it was wanted
Local authorities confirmed they could adopt a standardised colour system and that doing so would save them time. This validated the visual language work and proved that design standardisation at national scale was not only feasible, but genuinely welcomed.
03
Visual decisions must be grounded in the user's reality
An assumption that images would enrich the experience was challenged in testing. Planning officers found them unhelpful and accessibility teams raised concerns. The design was updated immediately , a reminder that visual choices only work when they serve real user needs.
Reflection

This was the richest, most complex discovery I’ve led , holding desk research, market analysis, user needs, technical constraints, and national policy context simultaneously, while keeping the team focused on the questions that would actually move the programme forward. It also taught me something about the long tail of design work: the impact of a discovery often lands long after the project closes, in places you’re not always watching.
